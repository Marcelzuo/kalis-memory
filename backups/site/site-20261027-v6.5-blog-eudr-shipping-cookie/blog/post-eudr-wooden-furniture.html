EUDR for Furniture Importers: What Changes Before Your Next Wooden Order Lands in the EU

Your container clears customs in Rotterdam. Two weeks later, it hasn't moved.

Your container clears customs in Rotterdam. Two weeks later, it hasn't moved.

Not because the paperwork is wrong. Because nobody in your supply chain can answer a question customs now asks: where exactly did the wood in these frames come from?

That question used to be an environmentalist's concern. It is now a customs checkpoint, and for anyone importing wooden furniture into the EU, it sits on your desk, not the factory's.

This is what the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) changes in practice — and what furniture retailers, interior designers, and hotel procurement teams should put in place before their next order ships.

The Paperwork Moved to Your Side of the Ocean

Here is the part that surprises most first-time importers: the factory cannot file this for you.

Under EUDR, the obligation sits with the operator placing the goods on the EU market. That is the importer of record — your company, or your EU buying entity. The due diligence statement that must accompany wooden furniture is yours to submit, and the liability for getting it wrong is yours to carry.

Your supplier in China can — and should — help you gather the evidence. But no factory export department can take the legal obligation off your hands. Teams that assume "the supplier handles compliance" are the ones whose containers sit at the border.

What Customs Will Actually Want to See

Strip away the legal language, and the evidence chain for a wooden shipment comes down to a few concrete things:

Geolocation of the harvest. The plots where the wood was grown or harvested need coordinates on file. Not the factory's address — the forest. For manufactured products like furniture, this information travels down the chain: forest to mill, mill to component supplier, component supplier to the factory that built your frames.

Traceability through every hand-off. If your sofa frames pass through a timber mill and a component workshop before reaching the assembly factory, each step needs documentation linking it to the last. One gap in the chain, and the whole statement behind it becomes unverifiable.

Proof of legal harvest. The wood must have been harvested legally in the country of origin. Land legality documents, harvesting permits, or the mill's own purchase records — the exact form varies by country and supplier, which is precisely why this is a documentation problem before it is a forestry problem.

A risk assessment you can defend. The importer must evaluate the risk that something in the chain is unverifiable, and show what was done about it. "We trusted the supplier" is not an assessment.

Why This Is a Sourcing Problem Before It Is a Legal One

Read that list again. Every single item is information that lives on the factory floor and in local supplier networks — not in your inbox in Europe.

This is where the regulation quietly reshapes how you should choose your supply chain.

A factory that has never been asked for plot-level geolocation data will not have it ready. A trader one step removed from production may not even know which mill supplied the timber. Neither is acting in bad faith — until an EU regulation makes that information mandatory, most suppliers simply have no reason to compile it.

The question for a buyer is not "is my factory good or bad." It is: can everyone in my chain produce this evidence on request, and has anyone verified that they actually can?

That is the same discipline as quality control, applied to paper instead of product. The checkpoints look familiar: ask before you order, verify while production runs, and hold the documentation until the container is sealed. If you already run mid-production QC, you have the access and the rhythm — you are extending an existing system, not building a new one.

What You Can Do Before Your Next Order Ships

If you have wooden furniture in production right now, or an order you are about to place, these steps cost nothing but time:

  1. Ask your supplier for the timber chain in writing. Who supplied the wood, which mill processed it, and what harvest documentation exists. The quality of the answer tells you more than any certificate on a website.
  2. Request geolocation data for the wood source. If the answer is confusion rather than coordinates, you have found your compliance risk early — before booking a vessel instead of after.
  3. Add a documentation clause to your purchase order. Make EUDR evidence a condition of the final balance. Suppliers prioritize what the order says.
  4. Start the file now, not at customs. Build the due diligence folder while production runs, so nothing depends on chasing documents from a factory that has already moved on to the next customer's order.

Four questions, sent this week, that either surface a problem you can still fix or build the evidence file customs will ask for.

The Bigger Shift

EUDR is not the last regulation that will push compliance work onto the importer's side. It is a signal of direction: the EU keeps moving paperwork, traceability, and liability toward the party that places goods on its market — and away from the party that makes them.

Importers who treat this as a one-off form to fill will keep getting caught at the border. Importers who build a supply chain that can produce evidence on demand turn the same regulation into a filter that removes less prepared competitors from the shelf.

The wood in your next container did not change. The questions at the border did.

FAQ

Q: Is a sourcing partner responsible for EUDR compliance on my orders?

A: No. The legal obligation stays with the EU importer of record. A sourcing partner's role is operational: helping collect the timber chain documentation, verifying supplier answers on the ground, and keeping the evidence file complete before shipment. The submission and the liability remain yours.

Q: Does EUDR apply only to solid wood furniture?

A: No. It covers products containing wood components — which in furniture means frames, panels, and composite wood parts. If your order includes any wooden element, it falls within scope. The definitive scope for your specific goods should be confirmed against the official legal text and, for complex cases, a customs specialist.
