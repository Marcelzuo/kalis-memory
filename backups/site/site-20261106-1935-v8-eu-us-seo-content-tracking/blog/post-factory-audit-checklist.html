Factory Audit Checklist for Furniture Buyers: What to Check Before You Place the First Order

A showroom visit tells you what a supplier wants photographed. An audit tells you who owns the machines, who signs off quality, and whether the approved sample can be repeated at order volume. Before the first order, the useful question is narrower than “is this a good factory”: can this supplier make your product, to your spec, more than once?

Factory or trading company?

Start with the legal entity. Ask for the business license and read the registered scope: a genuine manufacturer lists production and manufacturing activities, not only wholesale and trade. Then check that the address on the license matches where the machines are. A mismatch is not automatically fraud — but it demands an explanation.

Ask directly: which production lines are yours, and which steps are outsourced? A trading company can be a fine partner — you just need to know who is responsible when something goes wrong.

One practical test: propose an unannounced video call. A factory that owns its floor can show it in ten minutes. One that must "schedule" every call is telling you something.

The production floor

Walk the floor and check that the equipment matches the product. Solid wood furniture needs real woodworking machines — cutting, edge banding, CNC, sanding. Upholstered pieces need frame assembly, foam cutting, and sewing stations. Lacquered finishes need a spray booth and dust-controlled drying area. If a factory sells upholstered beds but you only see woodworking machines, someone else makes the soft parts.

Ask which key processes are subcontracted. Outsourced painting or sewing is common; the risk is that quality standards travel poorly between factories.

Then look at work in progress. Half-finished frames, stacked components, a schedule on the wall — signs the factory is real and busy. An empty floor on a weekday visit is a question worth asking.

Quality control in practice

Certificates are easy to show; habits are not. Look for a QC team that answers to someone other than the production manager — independent quality control must have authority to stop a line.

Ask to see records, not promises: a signed first-piece confirmation before mass production, in-process inspection logs, a documented way to handle defects (rework, scrap, or "ship it anyway"), and a final random inspection before packing. A factory that cannot produce one recent inspection record runs quality control on memory, not a system.

The audit is mostly a records check. Either the first-piece sign-off and in-process logs exist, or they do not.

The sample room

Ask who made the samples on the shelf. Some sample rooms hold pieces made once by the best worker in the building, never reproduced at line speed. Ask whether the sample came off the production line or a bench, and whether the factory keeps retained samples from past orders — a leave-sample system shows reproduction is part of the job, not an accident.

The right question: "If I reorder this piece in a year, who makes it, and from what reference?" If the answer is one craftsman's memory, that is your supply risk.

Commercial red flags

Slow down, or walk away, when you see these:

  • The contracting entity differs from the factory on the license, with no explanation.
  • The supplier only offers a catalogue and resists talking about production at all.
  • Video calls are refused, endlessly postponed, or always tightly scripted.
  • A quote far below every other offer — artificially low pricing gets paid for somewhere: materials, drying time, or QC.
  • Payment requested to a personal account instead of the company account.

Any one of these does not prove fraud. Together, they usually do.

A one-page audit checklist

  • [ ] Business license scope includes manufacturing, not only trade
  • [ ] License address matches the production site you visited
  • [ ] Factory can show its own machines for your product category
  • [ ] Outsourced processes identified in writing
  • [ ] Production floor active with work in progress during the visit
  • [ ] Independent QC function with authority to reject
  • [ ] First-piece confirmation and in-process inspection records exist
  • [ ] Final random inspection before shipment, documented with photos
  • [ ] Retained samples kept from previous orders
  • [ ] Company bank account matches the contracting entity

What to do with the answers

All boxes ticked: proceed, but start with a trial order. A trial order tests the relationship in a way a report cannot.

Most boxes ticked, gaps explainable: conditional pass. Write the missing controls into the purchase contract — inspection before balance payment, photo and video evidence at defined stages — and re-audit after the first shipment.

Core boxes failed (entity mismatch, refused video, no records): do not place the order. No price justifies it.

FAQ

Q: Is a video call enough to verify a Chinese supplier?

A: A video call is a screening tool, not an audit. It can confirm a floor exists; it cannot confirm QC records, retained samples, or that the same line will make your reorder. Use video as the first filter, and an in-person furniture factory inspection before your first significant order.

Q: Should I announce the audit in advance?

A: Announce the visit, but keep one element unannounced: ask for an ad-hoc video walk-through on a different day. A factory that only looks good on scheduled visit days is rehearsing.

Q: What should a factory audit report contain?

A: The entity check, photos of production lines and work in progress, outsourced processes listed, QC records reviewed, defects found, and a clear pass / conditional pass / fail conclusion. Where product regulations or labeling rules are involved, confirm requirements with your customs broker or legal advisor — an audit report is not compliance advice.

Before you place the first order

Treat the audit and the first order as one decision. Check the entity, walk the floor, read the records, then place a trial order.
