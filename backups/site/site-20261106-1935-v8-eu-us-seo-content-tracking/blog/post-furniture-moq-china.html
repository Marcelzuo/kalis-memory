Furniture MOQ from China: What Small Buyers Get Wrong About Minimum Order Quantities

Ask a Chinese furniture factory for its MOQ and you will get a number. The problem is that the number means nothing until you know how it was calculated. A quoted "50 pieces" might be per SKU, per fabric colour, or per container order — and each basis changes what you can actually buy.

MOQ is a scheduling decision before it is a price. The factory is asking whether your order is large enough to justify moving a line. Once you know which cost the minimum is covering, the number becomes something you can work with.

MOQ is quoted per SKU, per material, or per order

There are three common bases, and factories often mix them without saying so.

  • Per SKU: 30 pieces of one model, in any mix of colours the factory stocks.
  • Per material: 30 pieces total, but each fabric or veneer must reach its own minimum — often set by the upholstery or panel supplier, not the furniture factory.
  • Per order: any items you like, as long as the whole purchase clears a value or volume threshold.

Misreading the basis is the most common first-order mistake. A buyer hears "MOQ 50", plans 50 chairs across four fabrics, then learns each fabric needs 50. Always ask: "Is this minimum per item, per material, or per order?" before you build a plan around it.

Why factories set a minimum

Minimums cover setup cost. A small order still needs the same machine change, material purchase and finishing slot. Every order forces a factory to:

  • Set up machines and change tooling between runs
  • Buy or cut material in batch sizes (fabric rolls, veneer sheets, foam blocks)
  • Run a paint or lacquer batch — finishing lines are scheduled by colour, and a one-off colour wastes a whole chamber cycle
  • Allocate packaging materials and production-line time

A 20-piece order consumes most of the scheduling effort of a 200-piece order but recovers far less. That gap is the MOQ — so reduce the factory's setup burden and the number drops.

What actually moves the number

Levers that work, roughly in order of impact:

  1. Standard colours and stock fabrics. Custom RAL colours or special upholstery reset the material minimum. Choosing from the factory's existing palette often cuts MOQ sharply.
  2. Mixing within one series. Items sharing a frame, hardware or finish can often be pooled.
  3. Longer lead times. If your order can wait for a gap in the schedule, the factory loses nothing by squeezing it in.
  4. Paying sample or setup fees. A refundable or partly-refundable fee converts your small run into a covered trial.
  5. A stated trial-then-repeat plan. Factories will discuss a small first order when the buyer is credible and the reorder intent is real; exact quantities still depend on style and material.

The LCL math small buyers miss

A low MOQ does not mean a low landed cost. Small order furniture from China usually moves as LCL furniture shipping (less-than-container load), and LCL carries costs FCL buyers never see:

Before celebrating a 20-piece MOQ, ask your forwarder for an all-in LCL quote and compare cost per piece against a larger FCL order. Sometimes buying closer to a full container is cheaper per unit — and the "extra" stock turns over faster than the fees pile up.

How to ask for a real MOQ

Vague requests get vague answers. Send the factory (or your furniture sourcing agent) six things, and ask for a per-SKU quote:

  1. Product category — e.g. upholstered dining chair, oak veneer sideboard
  2. Dimensions — your spec or the factory's standard size
  3. Material — frame wood, panel type, fabric grade, foam density
  4. Finish — lacquer colour, standard palette vs custom
  5. Target quantity — a range, not a wish ("30–60 pieces for first order")
  6. Destination port and timeline — e.g. Rotterdam or Hamburg, needed on site by a given month

A supplier who replies with a per-SKU MOQ, packaging dimensions and CBM per piece is quoting seriously. A single lump number means guessing.

Red flags

Walk carefully if you see these:

  • MOQ quoted instantly, without asking what you're buying. It's a script, not a quote.
  • Refusal to make a sample, or samples promised "after full payment".
  • No clarity on whether packaging is included in price or MOQ.
  • One total price only — no unit price, no CBM, no packaging breakdown.
  • Pressure for 100% upfront. A normal structure is a deposit with the balance against pre-shipment evidence — for example, photos and video of the finished goods before the final payment, plus independent QC at the factory.

A realistic first-order path

Do not plan around a magic number like "30 pieces" or a fixed delivery date. Plan around a sequence:

  1. Trial order. Small, in standard materials and colours. Goal: verify quality, packaging and communication — not margin.
  2. Review. Check goods against your spec; document defects, fit, finish. Run QC before shipment, not after: photos and video before the balance is paid catch problems while they are still fixable.
  3. Repeat order. Now you have real data — actual lead time, CBM and defect rate. Scale quantity and negotiate MOQ from evidence.

Order one is a paid verification step. Budget for it that way.

FAQ

Q: Is the furniture MOQ China suppliers quote negotiable?

A: Yes, within limits. The minimum reflects real setup costs, so it moves when you reduce those costs — standard colours, mixed SKUs in one series, flexible timing. It rarely moves on insistence alone.

Q: Are small orders always more expensive per piece?

A: Usually, somewhat — you absorb more setup and LCL handling per unit. But the gap shrinks with standard materials, and a well-priced small order beats a large one you can't sell.

Q: Should I choose LCL or FCL?

A: Compare all-in cost per piece, not freight rate. LCL suits first orders and mixed SKUs. Once one model sells steadily, FCL's predictable per-unit cost usually wins. Freight by sea to major European ports is routine either way; ask for a door-or-port quote including destination charges before deciding.
