Importing Furniture from China to the US: A First-Order Checklist

A US furniture import runs on a different file from an EU one. There is no EORI and no import VAT to defer. Instead you need a bond, an ISF filed before the container leaves China, an HTS line that can change, and a product-safety position for the category. Six items decide whether the first container clears: importer of record, bond, ISF timing, HTS classification, the Lacey Act, and CPSC. Settle them before the deposit. A missing item shows up at the port, where fees run daily.

Importer of record, EIN, and the customs bond

The furniture importer of record (IOR) is the entity legally responsible for the entry — declaration, duties, compliance failures. Normally that is your own company. You need:

  • A US business entity, or a foreign entity qualified to import — a foreign company can act as IOR in some cases, but must appoint a US agent and carries the same liability.
  • An EIN — the IRS number that doubles as your customs importer number. Get it before booking, not after.
  • A customs bond. A single-entry bond covers one shipment; a continuous bond covers all entries for a year. One shipment a year, single-entry may be simpler; several containers, continuous usually costs less per entry.

The broker files the entry, but liability stays with the IOR. Appoint one early — most brokers will not touch a shipment they did not see coming.

ISF and entry: the clock starts before the ship sails

The paperwork starts before the vessel sails.

ISF ("10+2") is generally required to be filed at least 24 hours before the cargo is laden on the vessel at the Chinese port — ten data elements including seller, buyer, manufacturer, country of origin, and HTS number. Late or missing ISF can mean penalties, holds, or refused loading. Your supplier and forwarder supply most of the data; the IOR is responsible for making sure the filing happens on time.

After arrival, the broker files the entry (goods release) and entry summary (duty calculation) against your document set: commercial invoice with itemised descriptions (not "furniture"), unit prices, and Incoterm; packing list with piece counts and weights; bill of lading (check consignee and notify party before sailing); HTS classification for every line; and Lacey Act data for wood products.

A furniture sourcing agent can review this set before the container is sealed. Fixing an invoice in Foshan costs nothing; fixing it in Long Beach does not.

HTS classification and the tariff line

Every product enters under a code in the HTSUS. Furniture codes turn on material and function: an upholstered sofa, an oak table, and a metal-frame bed are three different lines with three different duty treatments. Two things catch first-time importers:

  1. There is no single "furniture duty rate." The base rate depends on the exact HTS code.
  2. Goods of Chinese origin may carry Section 301 duties on top of the base rate, and those lists and rates have changed over time and can change again. Never budget off a rate quoted for a different product — or for last year.

Classify each SKU, then get your broker's written confirmation of what applies at entry; for ambiguous items, a CBP binding ruling removes the guesswork. Do not accept a supplier's casual "the HS code is 9403" without checking the full ten-digit line.

Lacey Act: the wood question

The Lacey Act targets illegally harvested plants and plant products. Many wood-containing products, including wooden furniture, require a Lacey Act declaration at import, filed with the scientific name and country of harvest. Wood packaging used only to protect the cargo is normally outside the declaration, unless the packaging itself is the imported product.

Three things to get right:

  • Whether your specific HTS code triggers a declaration — your broker or APHIS guidance confirms this, product by product.
  • The supplier must state the species and harvest country in writing. "It's oak" is not an answer; a scientific name is. Shifting answers are a sourcing red flag.
  • FSC or any other certification does not make you Lacey-compliant automatically. It helps evidence legality; the declaration is still yours to file.

MDF and plywood raise the same questions. Ask the factory in writing before the order and keep the answer on file.

Product safety at the US border

US product safety is enforced by the CPSC, and the rules are category-specific — not one blanket regime for "furniture."

  • Mattresses and upholstered furniture may fall under federal flammability standards; compliance usually means tested components and correct labelling.
  • Children's furniture is regulated far more tightly: applicable standards, third-party testing, and a Children's Product Certificate can come into play.
  • Standard adult tables, chairs, and casegoods generally face fewer federal certification requirements — but state rules can still apply, California's flammability and chemical-labelling regimes being the best known.

The practical move: list your exact SKUs, check which CPSC requirements apply to each, and get written confirmation and test reports from the supplier before ordering. Do not let "we are CE certified" reassure you — CE is European and says nothing about US rules.

Port to warehouse

Los Angeles / Long Beach, Newark, and Savannah are the names you will see most on China–US furniture lanes; your forwarder confirms which serves your warehouse best. Between vessel arrival and your dock:

  1. Discharge and availability — released by CBP and available at the terminal.
  2. Chassis — a separate rental from the container, not always in supply at peak.
  3. Drayage — the short truck haul to your warehouse or a devanning facility.
  4. Detention and demurrage — free days are negotiated and run out fast; a missed dock appointment turns free time into fees.
  5. Dock appointment and unloading — book labour and equipment before pickup, not after.

A container can clear, release and still sit at the terminal because no one booked chassis, drayage or a dock appointment. That is an expensive way to learn the last mile is part of the import.

A first-order checklist

Before you pay the deposit:

  • ☐ Importer of record decided: your US entity (with EIN) or a qualified alternative
  • ☐ Customs bond chosen: single-entry vs continuous, priced by your broker
  • ☐ Broker appointed before booking — not at the port
  • ☐ ISF data collected; filing planned for ≥24 hours before loading
  • ☐ HTS classification agreed in writing for every SKU, including Section 301 exposure
  • ☐ Lacey Act position confirmed: declaration needed? Species and harvest country in hand?
  • ☐ CPSC requirements checked per category; test reports requested
  • ☐ Document set specified to the supplier (invoice, packing list, B/L)
  • ☐ Independent QC arranged — photo/video evidence before balance payment
  • ☐ Drayage, chassis, free days, and dock appointment planned

FAQ

Q: Do I need a continuous bond for my first import?

A: No. A single-entry bond covers one shipment and is a normal starting point. Expect multiple shipments within a year? Ask your broker to price a continuous bond — it is often cheaper per entry.

Q: Can my freight forwarder act as importer of record?

A: Some offer IOR services, but understand what you are buying: the goods enter under a third party's identity, and control of declarations and compliance records shifts with it. For a serious programme, your own entity as furniture importer of record is the cleaner foundation.

Q: Is there a US tariff on furniture from China?

A: Yes — a base duty under the HTSUS, and goods of Chinese origin may also carry Section 301 duties depending on the exact code and current lists. Rates change; confirm against your specific HTS classification at entry.
