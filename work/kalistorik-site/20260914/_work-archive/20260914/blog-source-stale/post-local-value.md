# You Visit China Twice a Year. Your Orders Run All Twelve Months.

You land, shake hands, walk the showroom, sign off a sample, wire the deposit — and twelve hours later you are back in the air, heading home. The order lives on as a PDF in your inbox.

Then comes the part nobody warns you about: the long quiet stretch between that handshake and the container arriving at your warehouse. Months of production, scheduling, packing and loading — all of it happening on the other side of the planet, while you run a shop, answer customers and sleep at night.

Distance itself is not the problem. Plenty of European retailers source successfully from China without living there. The problem is what distance does to control. When nobody with your interests stands on the factory floor, small drifts become big ones, and big ones become claims, markdowns and missed seasons.

This piece is about that gap: what actually happens inside it, what it costs, and what closes it.

## The Gap Nobody Warns You About

First-time importers imagine the gap as a waiting room. Deposit paid, production running, ship date confirmed — nothing to do but wait.

That is not what the gap is. The gap is an active period where dozens of decisions get made about your order: which batch of fabric gets cut first, whose order jumps the queue when a machine breaks down, whether a substitute hardware kit is "close enough," which week your packing actually happens.

Every one of those decisions gets made by someone. The only question is whether anyone representing you is in the room when they are made.

Factories are not villains here. They are professionals running many orders at once under real constraints, and they will solve problems the way that makes sense from inside their own operation. When your buyer is nine time zones away and answers email once a day, "sensible for the factory" and "sensible for you" drift apart — politely, quietly, and without anyone deciding to let it happen.

A warm relationship from your last visit does not survive that drift on its own. The factory that hosted you so well in spring may take a week to answer your Thursday email in autumn. Not out of disrespect — your message is simply one of hundreds, and it has no feet.

## What Actually Happens When Nobody Is Watching

Three patterns show up again and again in orders that go wrong during the quiet months.

**One: the queue reshuffles.** A production date is not a protected slot unless someone treats it as one. A larger order arrives, an urgent reorder lands, a machine goes down — and your batch slides. The factory may still tell you "production is on track," because technically something with your specs is in progress. Meanwhile, the calendar has moved, and nobody on your side knows until the booking date arrives and slips.

**Two: the sample and the batch diverge.** Your approved sample was made with care, by the best hands in the workshop, from carefully selected materials. Mass production runs on different shifts, with different lumber lots, different dye batches, different humidity in the finishing room. None of that is cheating — it is physics and production reality. But without someone comparing live output against your approved sample while the line is running, divergence only surfaces at unboxing. By then it is too late to fix and too expensive to return.

**Three: the paperwork says fine, the floor says otherwise.** Status updates from a distance describe intentions, not reality. "Packing next week" is a plan. A timestamped photo of your cartons on the loading floor is a fact. Buyers who rely on the first and never see the second are, in practice, managing their supply chain on faith.

None of these three patterns is dramatic on any given day. That is exactly why they survive. Each one is a small, reasonable-looking event that only adds up to a disaster when you look at the whole timeline at once — usually from the wrong end, in front of an open container.

## What One Missed Season Really Costs

Ask a retailer what a delayed container costs and they will start with freight. That is the smallest line on the bill.

The real damage is structural. Furniture retail runs on seasons and shelf windows. Goods that miss the launch window do not just arrive late — they arrive into a demand curve that has already peaked and turned. What was full-price inventory becomes clearance inventory. What was a planned reorder becomes a hesitation.

Then there is the customer layer. Regulars who found empty shelves do not file complaints; they simply shop elsewhere next time. You will never see that loss on an invoice, and it is the most expensive part of the whole episode.

And there is the negotiation layer. A batch that arrives wrong becomes a cross-time-zone claims process — emails, photos, partial credits, promises of discounts on a future order. Recovery, when it comes at all, is a fraction of the loss. Everyone who has been through it knows the arithmetic: the cost of one failed order dwarfs the cost of having someone on the ground for a whole year.

That is the uncomfortable math behind the "do it yourself to save money" instinct. Self-managed sourcing looks cheaper right up until the season it isn't, and then it is more expensive than any service fee could ever have been.

## What a Local Partner Does Before Problems Happen

The value of an on-the-ground partner is easy to describe wrongly, as "extra QC." It is broader than that. It is presence — someone with your spec in hand, your authority delegated, and the language and relationships to act in real time.

Concretely, across the quiet months:

- **The production schedule gets verified, not trusted.** When shipment week approaches and the factory proposes "push to next week," your partner is standing in the lobby asking what has actually been produced, what is packed, and what the revised plan means for your vessel booking. You get a conclusion the same day, not an email thread that ages for a week.

- **The line gets walked while it runs.** Live video from the production floor shows your actual batch — not the showroom, not the sample room. Discrepancies get flagged while a handful of pieces are affected and fixes are still cheap. That is the entire difference between correction and claim.

- **Decisions get made at factory speed.** A material substitution, a simplified process step, a rescheduled slot — these questions cannot wait for your morning. Someone on the floor who knows your non-negotiables can answer them the same day, and escalate to you only what genuinely requires your call.

- **Loading gets witnessed.** Carton counts, labelling, stacking condition, the seal number — verified in person before the doors close. After the container leaves the yard, every one of those things becomes an argument. Before it leaves, they are a checklist.

When trouble does emerge — and in this industry it eventually does, for everyone — the difference is even starker. A face-to-face conversation between people who will see each other again next month moves a factory manager. A video call from a buyer who may never return does not. Relationship continuity is leverage, and only presence builds it.

## How to Test Your Current Setup This Week

You do not need to change partners or sign anything to find out whether your gap is covered. Run this test before your next order:

1. **Ask your factory, in writing, to confirm four things:** the mid-production inspection date, the date you will receive production photos of your actual batch, the packing week, and who from your side is expected on-site at loading.

2. **Watch what comes back.** A supplier used to buyer-side presence will answer without friction. Vague replies — "don't worry, we handle QC internally" — tell you exactly how much enforcement your commitments will get later.

3. **Check your last order's evidence trail.** Do you have timestamped production-floor photos? A witnessed loading record? A seal number captured before departure? If your only documentation is what the factory volunteered, you already know the answer.

4. **Time one question.** Ask a mid-production question on a Tuesday and note when the real answer — not the reassuring answer — arrives. Response latency is the single best predictor of how your order will behave when something goes slightly wrong.

Small retailers consistently underestimate how much of this they can demand. You do not need volume to ask for written checkpoints. You need the willingness to ask, and someone to follow up when the answer is soft. ## The Honest Comparison

Two ways to cover the gap exist, and only two.

You can cover it yourself: more trips, longer visits, someone from your team relocating for production windows. That works. It is also the most expensive option on this list once you count flights, days out of the shop, and the fact that your presence still only covers the weeks you are physically there.

Or you can cover it with a local partner who is there during the weeks you are not — who walks the floor when your batch runs, answers the factory's same-day questions, and stands at the container when it loads. Between your visits, the order never goes unwatched.

What does not work is the third option everyone defaults to: covering the gap with optimism. Email check-ins, WhatsApp updates and a good feeling from last spring's visit. The gap does not care about your relationship. It only responds to presence.

Freight is where most buyers first feel the consequences — a production slip of one week becomes a rolled booking, and rolled bookings in peak season become weeks. The connection between floor-level control and landed timing is direct, which is why we treat sourcing and [freight](https://kalistorik.com/freight) as one chain rather than two problems.

Your next factory visit will be warm, useful and entirely insufficient on its own. The question worth sitting with is not "did I choose a good factory?" You probably did. The question is: in the months after you fly home, who is you?

## FAQ

**Q: I only order once or twice a year. Is an on-the-ground partner still worth it for orders that small?**
A: The gap between deposit and loading is the same length regardless of order size, and small buyers are more exposed per order, not less — one bad container is a larger share of your annual stock. Presence is usually engaged per order or per key checkpoint rather than as a permanent retainer, so even a couple of orders a year can be covered at the moments that matter.

**Q: My factory sends me photos and updates regularly. Isn't that enough?**
A: Volunteer photos show what the factory wants you to see, taken when it is convenient for them. Verified evidence means timestamped photos and video of your actual batch on the running line, requested at defined checkpoints and compared against your approved sample. The difference only becomes visible when something has gone wrong — which is precisely when it is too late.

**Q: Won't bringing in a local partner offend my factory?**
A: Professional factories work with buyer-side representatives constantly; in the major furniture regions it is completely normal and often welcomed, because it means questions get answered faster and payment triggers are clearer. Offence only arises with suppliers who were counting on nobody checking. Their reaction is itself useful information.

**Q: What should I delegate to a local partner versus keep for myself?**
A: Keep commercial decisions — pricing, order changes, final acceptance of anything outside spec. Delegate verification and speed: schedule checks, mid-production inspections, substitution judgements measured against your approved sample, loading supervision. Write the boundary down before the order starts; a light, explicit mandate outperforms both total control and total trust.

**Q: How do I know if the problem was distance or just a bad factory?**
A: You usually cannot tell from one order — a bad outcome looks identical from the outside. What you can do is change one variable: keep the factory, add presence, and compare the evidence trail. Orders that improve with presence were distance problems. Orders that still fail with someone on the floor were supplier problems, and now you have the documentation to act on.
